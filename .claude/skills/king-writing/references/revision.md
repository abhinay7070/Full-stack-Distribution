# Revision: door shut, door open

> Origin (John Gould, King's first editor, at a weekly paper): when you write a story you're telling yourself the story.
> When you rewrite, your main job is taking out everything that is not the story.
> Write with the door closed; rewrite with the door open.

## 1. The cycle

| Stage | Door | What you do | What you never do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Draft 1, the "All-Story" draft** | Shut | Write as fast as is comfortable, to outrun self-doubt. Look back only to check names and facts. | Show it to anyone, or ask "what does the green dress mean?" |
| **Rest** | Shut | Novel: at least 6 weeks in a drawer. Write something short and different in between. | Peek at it. If it reads well you'll want to keep going; if it reads badly you'll tinker too early. |
| **Read-through** | Shut | Read it in one sitting if possible, pencil in hand. Fix typos, inconsistencies and **holes big enough to drive a truck through**. Don't beat yourself up. | Leave errors for the copyeditor. Only a slob does that. |
| **Draft 2** | Open | Make the theme clearer. Cut 10%+. Fix the back story. Add scenes that reinforce the meaning. | Keep material because it's "good". It should be good, since you're paid for that. You aren't paid to be self-indulgent. |
| **First readers** | Open | Ideal Reader first, then 4-8 trusted readers. | Revise to please one outlier. |
| **Polish** | Open | Close to a third draft. | |

King's own rhythm: **two drafts and a polish.** Writers vary; Vonnegut perfected each page as he went. For a beginner: at least two drafts, one door-shut and one door-open.

## 2. The read-through: two levels at once

**Top of the mind (toolbox chores):**
- Knock out pronouns with unclear antecedents.
- Add clarifying phrases where they're needed.
- Delete every adverb you can bear to part with (never all of them; never enough).

**Underneath (the Big Questions):**
- Is the story coherent? What would turn coherence into a song?
- What are the recurring elements, and do they make a theme?
- What did I mean? Add what reinforces it and cut what points somewhere else (often the flailing near the start).
- What I want most is **resonance**: something that lingers after the reader closes the book. No spoon-fed moral.
- Most common big error: **character motivation**. Note it like this: "p.91, X does Y. Why? X would NEVER do this."

## 3. The formula
**2nd Draft = 1st Draft − 10%.** It came from a handwritten note on a rejection slip: "Not bad, but PUFFY."
- Every story can be cut down. If you can't remove 10% and keep the story and its flavor, you aren't trying hard enough.
- King is a natural "putter-inner". The formula is the counterweight. Examples: 4,000 → 3,600 words; 350,000 → 315,000 (300,000 if possible).
- Judicious cutting has an immediate effect. You'll feel it, and your reader will too.

### Guardrail: the formula serves the story, never the reverse
- The 10% applies to a **first draft** (door shut, puffy). A text that was already revised may have little left to cut. Then cut less, and say why.
- Every cut must remove something that is not the story (repetition, the obvious, stage directions, fear words, explaining that does the reader's thinking).
  **Never cut** a true qualifier ("almost nobody" is not "nobody"), a load-bearing detail (the blue 8), the writer's voice line, or texture that makes a person real.
- Every change is either **style** (same meaning, better form) or **content** (new fact, removed fact, changed claim). Style changes are the skill's job.
  Content changes need a reason the writer can veto, and every new fact gets a [CONFIRM] tag until it's checked.
- If a cut changes what the text claims, revert it.

## 4. Edit taxonomy (reverse-engineered from King's annotated "1408" revision)
Tag each change in your change log with one of these:

| # | Edit type | King's example | Generalized rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | **Placeholder title → real title** | "The Hotel Story" became "1408" (a 13th-floor story whose digits add to 13) | A title should carry a hidden pattern or promise |
| E2 | **Shorter names** | "Ostermeyer" became "Olin", saving ~15 lines | Long names and labels cost space every time they appear. Shorten them globally. |
| E3 | **Stop doing the reader's thinking** | 5 lines cut to 2 | Readers can infer, so cut the explanation |
| E4 | **Cut stage directions, the obvious, and clumsy back story** | "Out it goes" | If it belabors, delete it |
| E5 | **Plant props early** | The lucky Hawaiian shirt moved from page ~30 to the opening | Chekhov's gun works in reverse: what pays off at the end must be planted early, or it looks like a deus ex machina |
| E6 | **Cut "well, duh" actions and trite lines** | "Sat down in one of the chairs" (where else?) and "They're Cuban" cigars | If no other choice was possible, don't narrate it. Cut lines every bad movie uses. |
| E7 | **Cut to the bone, kill the adverb** | "shortly" stomped | Keep the information and drop the words |
| E8 | **A deliberate exception** | Kept "said heartily" because it signals mockery | Keep a rule-break only when you can name its job |
| E9 | **Cut repetition, add a line that clarifies character** | Repeated text removed; a line added about a man at ease in his own room | Revision adds as well as cuts, when the addition sharpens character |

Most edits are cuts made to speed the story (Strunk: omit needless words).
The first draft is the story in its socks and underwear. The revision is the story dressed, hair combed, with a dash of cologne.

## 5. Ideal Reader (I.R.)
- **All writing is a letter to one person.** Name that person before drafting.
- **Door shut:** I.R. is in the room in spirit. You bend the story toward them before they've read a word. This is how you play to an audience that isn't there yet.
- **Door open:** ask your questions out loud, and watch where I.R. puts the pages down. What was so easy to stop reading?
- **I.R. checks pace, back story, and whether a scene is funny or scary enough yet.** If you have something with potential, twist it as hard as you can.
- **Example:** King's wife asked "who cares?" about two pages of a character's community-service work. King cut them to two paragraphs, and no reader ever missed them.
- **When a novelist answers criticism with "Yeah, but...", it's a darling talking.**

## 6. Handling feedback

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| All readers like it | You probably did a good job |
| Readers split (A works for half, B for the other half) | **It's a wash. A tie goes to the writer.** Leave it. |
| Everyone flags the same problem | **You have a problem. Fix it.** |
| A factual error with its fix | Free win: you look like an expert and the reader feels helpful |
| Vague praise or critique ("I loved the tone thing...") | Ignore it. It's wind with no facts in it. |
| I.R. versus others | Weight I.R. most heavily |

## 7. Pace
- **Pace is the speed at which the narrative unfolds.** "Faster sells" is an unexamined myth. Let each story find its own pace.
  Too fast loses the reader; too slow makes even patient readers restless.
- **Elmore Leonard's rule: leave out the parts readers skip.** Usually that means cutting (kill your darlings).
- **King's chronic failing is overexplaining.** Check yours.

## 8. Back story
- **Back story is everything that happened before the tale began and affects it.** Get it in fast, with grace.
  - Graceless: "Hello, ex-wife," Tom said.
  - Graceful: his fingers creep to where the wedding ring used to be. A gesture carries the fact.
- **King prefers telling the story from A to Z over starting in medias res,** because flashbacks feel corny to him. What *happens next* matters more than what already happened.
- **Everyone has a history, and most of it isn't interesting.** Keep the parts that are. Long life stories belong in bars, late, and only if you're buying.
- **If I.R. doesn't understand a piece of back story,** ask whether *you* understand it. If you do, clarify it in draft 2. If you don't, think harder about the past.

## 9. Research = back story
- **It belongs in the back.** It's a pinch of spice in the sauce, there to add verisimilitude.
- **Draft with the door shut and make up what you don't know.** Fix the "howlers" later with real research.
- **Don't let the tail wag the dog.** You're writing a story, not a research paper.

## 10. Critique protocol (when asked to review someone's draft)
1. **Read it straight through once, as I.R.** Note where attention drops.
2. **Big questions:** coherence, motivation, theme, holes, back story, pace.
3. **Toolbox pass:** adverbs, passive voice, dialogue tags, inflated words, clichés, unclear pronouns, paragraph shape.
4. **Run `scripts/king_lint.py`** (if a shell is available).
5. **Give specific notes, never vague ones.** Each note names the line, the problem and the fix.
6. **Show the revised version with the cut %.** Target 2nd draft = 1st − 10%.
