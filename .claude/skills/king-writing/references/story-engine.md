# Story Engine (what to write and how a story is built)

## 0. What writing is: telepathy
The writer sends a picture across space and time. King's demo is a table, a red cloth, a caged rabbit, and a blue **8** on its back.
- **Give the rough frame and let the reader fill it.** The shade of red doesn't matter. Exact dimensions turn prose into an instruction manual.
- **Put the precision on the one detail that carries the meaning.** That is the 8. Not a six, not nineteen-point-five.
  → In any text, find the "blue 8": the single specific detail the reader must receive exactly.

## 1. What to write: anything, as long as you tell the truth
- **Read "write what you know" broadly.** The heart and the imagination know things too.
- **Start with what you love to read.** Don't write to impress your circle, and don't aim at a genre because it sells. It's dishonest, and it doesn't work.
  You can't aim a book like a cruise missile.
- **Blend in your own knowledge of life, work, friendship and sex. Especially work:** people love reading about work.
  A plumber who loves science fiction should consider plumbers in space.
- **Use what you know to enrich the story, never to lecture.** Grisham told the truth about young lawyers. Copy his directness, not his genre.
- **Be brave.** Map the enemy's positions, come back, and tell us everything you know.

## 2. The three parts, plus the thing King distrusts

| Part | Job |
|---|---|
| **Narration** | Moves the story from A to B to Z |
| **Description** | Makes a sensory reality the reader takes part in |
| **Dialogue** | Brings characters to life through their speech |
| ~~Plot~~ | The jackhammer. It breaks as much as it digs up. The good writer's last resort and the dullard's first choice. |

### Stories are fossils
Stories are found things that already exist. The writer digs them out with the gentlest tools that work.
Story is honorable and trustworthy. Plot is shifty and best kept under house arrest.

### The situation method (King's default)
1. **Situation first.** It's as stark as a store-window display: a few characters stuck in a predicament.
2. **Characters next.** They start flat and fill in as you go.
3. **Narrate and watch.** Don't steer them to safety. Write down what they do.
4. **You are the first reader.** If you can't guess the ending, neither can the reader. That's how you build page-turning anxiety.
5. **Every story comes out somewhere.** Stop trying to control the ending.

**Express the seed as a What-if.** Some of King's own seeds:
- What if vampires invaded a small New England village?
- What if a mother and her son were trapped in a stalled car by a rabid dog?
- Two characters in a house. One woman in a bedroom. One kid lost in the woods.

**Combining unrelated ideas sparks the seed.** Carrie = adolescent cruelty + telekinesis.
**Invert the expected.** King's exercise reverses the sexes in a stalker story, so a stale headline becomes fresh.

### Exercise template (use it to generate)
```
SEED:      [common situation everyone recognizes]
INVERSION: [flip one role, sex, power or expectation]
WHAT-IF:   What if ...?
CAST:      2-3 people, flat for now
NARRATE:   5-6 pages, no outline. Be honest about how each one talks and acts.
```

## 3. Description: how much, and what
- **It starts in the writer's imagination and finishes in the reader's.** Describe too much and you freeze out the reader's own picture.
- **Thin description bewilders the reader. Overdescription buries them.** It's easier to overdescribe.
- **Use a few well-chosen details that stand for everything else.** Usually these are the **first ones you see**.
- **Method:** a short, intense memory search with all the senses. Pick about 4 details: light, floor, walls, smell.
  Put them in, then get back to the story. A meal is as good as a feast.
- **Locale and texture beat physical inventories of people.** No catalogue of wardrobe. No "sharply intelligent blue eyes" as a shortcut to character.
- **Similes are one of fiction's chief delights.** A good one is like meeting an old friend in a crowd of strangers.
  - **Zen simile** (the connection doesn't land): the reader closes the book.
  - **Cliché simile** ("fought like a tiger"): looks lazy or ignorant.
  - **The target:** fresh, compact and precise, like hardboiled detective lines.
- **Formula:** clear seeing + clear writing (fresh images, simple vocabulary). Say what you see, then get on with the story.

## 4. Dialogue
- **Dialogue is sneaky.** It reveals character, often in ways the speaker doesn't know. Only what people *do* tells us more.
- **Show through speech; don't tell through narration.** King's example: a man who mangles "appetite" shows us his schooling. Nobody needs to state it.
- **Good dialogue is an honest-to-God exchange,** not a stilted soliloquy. Elmore Leonard is the model. Lovecraft-style elliptical monologue is the anti-model.
- **Clichéd war-movie lines** make the reader impatient and incredulous.
- **The key is honesty.** If a character would swear, let them swear. A prim aunt might say the polite version, and that choice tells us about her.
  Censoring your characters breaks the writer-reader contract.
- **The only question is how it rings on the page and in the ear.** To get it, talk yourself, and above all **shut up and listen** to others.

## 5. Character
- **Watch how real people behave, then tell the truth about what you see.**
- **Nobody is "the bad guy" in their own life.** Everyone is the protagonist and the camera is on them. Write villains who think they're sane and heroic.
- **"If I have to tell you, I lose."** Show the depressed woman compulsively eating cake. Don't write "she was depressed."
- **Every character is partly you:** what you'd do (or wouldn't), plus traits you've observed, plus pure blue-sky imagination.
- **Ordinary good people are harder to make vivid than villains.** Give the decent hero a flaw or a quirk, like a childish sense of humor, so he isn't a plaster saint.
- **Establish danger early with one act.** The smiling salesman kills a dog when nobody is looking.
- **Villains feel doubt and pity, and heroes try to turn away.** Then characters come alive and start doing things on their own.
- **The best stories end up being about the people, not the event.** But beyond short length, the story is the boss.

## 6. Bells and whistles
Use anything that improves the work and doesn't get in the story's way: alliteration, present tense, stream of consciousness.
Try it. If it doesn't work, delete it, even if you love it. **Murder your darlings** (Quiller-Couch).
Ornaments usually show up after the storytelling is done.

## 7. Symbolism and theme (second-draft work)
- **Symbolism is also part of the fossil.** If you find it on the reread, polish it like a jeweler. If it isn't there, so what? You still have the story.
  It exists to adorn and enrich, never to fake profundity.
- **Carrie example:** blood appears at the beginning, the climax and the end. King noticed it in the reread and brought it out.
- **Theme:** when the first draft is done, step back from the trees and look at the forest. Ask *what's it all about?*
  Make that clearer in draft 2. Add what reinforces it and delete what points elsewhere.
- **Good fiction starts with story and moves to theme, never the reverse.** Starting from the message is a recipe for bad fiction.
- **Theme is a tool,** like a magnifying glass. It also cures being stuck: ask *what am I really writing about?*
- **No moral is needed.** If the theme is clear, readers draw their own conclusions. "I don't know" can be the honest last line.

## 8. Writer's block (King's cure, from The Stand)
1. Don't abandon a big investment.
2. Take long walks. Allow boredom; it is fertile.
3. Circle the problem until the answer arrives whole ("thinking above the curve").
4. The fix is often the same move as the opening: a quick, hard cut through the knot.
5. Diagnose afterwards. The block usually means the story was betraying its own truth.
