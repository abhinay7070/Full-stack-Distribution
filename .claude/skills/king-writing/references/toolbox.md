# The Toolbox (sentence level)

King's metaphor: his grandfather's carpenter's toolbox. Common tools go on top. Carry the whole box so a surprise job
doesn't discourage you. Build at least 4 levels. Keep it light enough to carry.

| Level | Tools | Rule of thumb |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (top) | Vocabulary, grammar | Pack what you have, without shame. Clean the rust off. |
| 2 | Elements of style (Strunk & White) | Omit needless words. Active voice. Put the emphatic words at the end, *in moderation*. |
| 3 | Paragraph, form | The paragraph is the basic unit of writing. Learn its beat. |
| 4+ | Narration, description, dialogue, character, theme, pace | See story-engine.md and revision.md. |

---

## 1. Vocabulary
- **Don't dress it up.** Reaching for long words because you're ashamed of short ones is like putting a household pet in evening clothes.
- **First word that comes to mind**, if it's appropriate and colorful. If you stop and cogitate you'll find another word, but it is rarely closer to what you mean.
- **Meaning is the whole game.** Writing always falls short of the full meaning, so never pick a word that is only a cousin of the one you want.
- **Appropriateness still counts.** Plain doesn't have to mean crude. Choose the plain register your audience accepts.
- **Simple vocabulary can carry complex structure.** King's Steinbeck count: a 50-word sentence with 39 one-syllable words and none longer than two syllables. Keep the structure rich and the words small.
- **Don't consciously grow your vocabulary.** Reading does it for you.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| emolument | tip |
| perform an act of excretion | (the plain verb your audience accepts) |
| utilize / facilitate / commence | use / help / start |
| at this point in time / at the end of the day | now / (cut) |
| the fact that | (rebuild the sentence) |

## 2. Grammar
- **Noun + verb = sentence**, every time: "Rocks explode." "Jane transmits." When you're lost in clauses, grab this pole and stand up.
- **Fragments are allowed.** Strunk says the best writers sometimes break the rules, but unless you are certain of doing well, follow them. Break a rule on purpose, never from ignorance.
- **Bad grammar produces bad sentences.** A dangling modifier makes the meaning absurd. Check what each modifier attaches to.

## 3. Passive voice (avoid it)
- **Why writers use it:** timidity. It feels safe, and it looks like it has authority. So do instruction manuals and legal briefs.
- **Fix:** put the actor in charge. "The meeting will be held at seven" → "The meeting's at seven."
- **Acceptable:** when the actor is unknown or irrelevant. Even then, prefer a named actor: "Freddy and Myra carried the body..."
- **Splitting helps.** A tangled passive line often reads better as two short active sentences.
- **The reader is the main concern.** Strunk's image: the reader is floundering in a swamp, and your job is to throw a rope.
  The writer threw the rope. The rope wasn't thrown by the writer.

## 4. Adverbs (not your friend)
- **Why writers use them:** fear that the reader won't get it.
- **Test:** if the prose before "He closed the door firmly" already shows the mood, "firmly" is redundant. Cut it.
  If it doesn't, fix the context or the verb ("slammed").
- **They spread like dandelions.** One looks pretty. A week later the lawn is covered.
- **Dialogue attribution is the hard line.** Use "said". Swifties ("cried bravely", "said modestly") turn into party games.
  Steroid verbs ("grated", "gasped", "jerked out") are the same disease. Don't.
- **Model to copy:** Larry McMurtry's he-said/she-said, even in emotional crisis.
- **Dumbo's feather.** You don't need the adverb any more than Dumbo needed the feather. The magic was already in you.

## 5. Elements of style (level 2)
- **Possessives:** always add 's (Thomas's bike).
- **Emphasis:** Strunk puts the most important words at the end of the sentence. King says use judgment: "He killed Frank with a hammer" beats "With a hammer he killed Frank."
- **Omit needless words** (Strunk Rule 17). This is King's whole revision ethic in three words.
- **Pronouns are slippery.** During revision, kill any pronoun with an unclear antecedent.

## 6. Paragraphs (level 3): maps of intent
- **The look tells the reader what's coming before they read a word.** Lots of white space and short paragraphs = an easy, fast read. Packed blocks = a hard read.
- **Expository writing:** topic sentence, then support. This forces organized thinking; writing is refined thinking.
- **Fiction:** the paragraph is the beat, not the melody. While drafting, don't plan breaks; fix them in rewrite.
- **The single-sentence paragraph** sounds like talk. It varies pace, gives stage direction, and marks a transition.
  Use it where the beat turns. Overusing fragments is a fault too.
- **Telegraphic fragments** ("Tall masts, the sails tied.") can streamline narration and build tension. Don't overdo them.
- **Writing is seduction**, and good talk is part of seduction. Language doesn't always need to wear a tie.
- **Coherence starts at the paragraph.** That is where words begin to breathe.

## 7. Commitment
- **Come to the page any way but lightly:** nervous, angry, hopeful, all fine. Not casually.
- **Good writing means letting go of fear and affectation** and making good choices about which tools to use.
