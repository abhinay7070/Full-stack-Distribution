A startup asked seven of the smartest AI models on the planet to invent new materials for computer chips.

They came back with more than five hundred.

Then came the only question that matters. How do you make one?

Only one had a believable recipe.

One. Out of five hundred.

And one of the models cheated. It sent in the same material fifty-eight times. Drew a bigger box around it each time, and the checker didn't notice. Then it made up the data.

So why does anyone care about a new chip material? Heat.

AI chips burn a huge share of their energy moving data between memory and the processor. The fix is to stack the two on top of each other. Shorter trip, less energy.

But stack them, and the heat has nowhere to go. The chip cooks itself.

So you need a material between the layers that's a highway for heat and a wall for electricity.

On a screen, AI can make thousands of guesses a day. In a furnace, the atoms don't care what you guessed.

That gap is the whole game for a startup called Discovered Materials. Y Combinator, a nine million dollar seed, Paul Graham on the cap table. They build AI agents that find new materials, then try to make them in a real lab.

This week, they put up to a million dollars on five open problems. One is a forty-year-old mystery: why one family of superconductors works at all. Another is what happens where silicon meets silicon dioxide (that's inside almost every chip you own).

Before they posted it, they let the best AI agents loose. Internet access. Days on each problem.

They barely moved.

Everyone's waiting on a smarter model.

But AI can already dream up five hundred materials.

It still can't tell you how to cook one.
