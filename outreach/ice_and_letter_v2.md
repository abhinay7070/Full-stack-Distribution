## Email 1

```
Rohan,

In February 1806 a ship left Boston for Martinique carrying ice. Only ice.

Frederic Tudor, 22, had cut it from a pond and was sure the Caribbean would pay for it.

He was right about the ice. It made the trip.

He was wrong about everything else. Almost nobody on the island had ever had a cold drink, and you can't want what you've never tasted. He made ice cream in public to show them. It didn't matter. The ice melted. A few years later he was in debtors' prison.

The product was fine. The market was real. Nobody reached for it.

What fixed it was small. Almost embarrassing. I'll tell you tomorrow.

Abhinay

P.S. No link, no attachment. Just a story I keep coming back to.
```

## Email 2

```
Rohan,

The fix was bartenders.

Tudor went to the bars with a deal: he'd supply the ice, and they'd sell cold drinks at the same price as warm ones.

People tried one. Then they didn't want warm ones again. Soon the bars wanted more, and so did everyone else.

He never convinced the city. He convinced a few bartenders.

In 1833 his ice sailed around Africa to Calcutta. Four months at sea. The Tuscany left Boston with 180 tons and arrived with about 100. Calcutta became the most profitable place he ever sold to.

A ship from America to India, full of something nobody had asked for.

Tomorrow, a letter that went the other way.

Abhinay
```

On 16 January 1913, a clerk in the accounts office of the Madras Port Trust posted a letter to a stranger at Cambridge.

He had no degree. He earned £20 a year. He said both in the first lines. Then came pages of formulas.

G. H. Hardy nearly set it aside. That evening he went through it again with Littlewood. Some of the formulas, Hardy wrote later, had to be true, because no one would have had the imagination to invent them.

The clerk was Srinivasa Ramanujan. A year later he was at Cambridge.

Hardy didn't care where the letter came from. Only what was in it.

I'm writing from India too, and I'm no Ramanujan. But these emails have a reason. Tomorrow.

Abhinay
